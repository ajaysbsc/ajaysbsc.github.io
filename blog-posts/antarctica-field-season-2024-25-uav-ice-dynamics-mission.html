Chasing the Antarctic Dream: From Proposal Panic to East Antarctica

February 22, 2025 Antarctica

It all started with a small dream—so small, in fact, it could fit on a snowflake. Years ago, while sitting in a lecture hall at JNU, I watched someone give a presentation about their trip to Antarctica. It wasn’t a blockbuster movie or some glossy National Geographic special. Just a person, a few slides, and a lot of ice. But it struck me. From that day on, a thought camped in my head like a stubborn penguin: Someday, I want to go to Antarctica.

The Proposal That Almost Slipped Away

PhD life has a way of blurring months into a steady loop of coding, field campaigns and reviewer rebuttals. The idea of travelling to Antarctica lived quietly in the background—until a casual corridor chat with Professor RAAJ. Instead of smiling it off, he asked a single question that jolted me awake: “Have you checked whether NCPOR has an open call?”

We rushed to the website and the dream almost vaporised. The proposal deadline was that very day. Just as it seemed lost to the Southern Ocean, a notice appeared—ten-day extension. That was it: ten days to frame a compelling plan. Tirthankar, Shantan and I commandeered the department seminar room, fuelled by chai and optimism, and began to write a UAV-based glacier monitoring proposal.

The concept focussed on flying drones near Bharati Station, India’s younger Antarctic base. A quick reality check arrived from the logistics team: no helicopter support that season meant shifting to Maitri in East Antarctica. We rewrote fast, hit submit, and a few weeks later the word came back—accepted.

Rules, Seats, and Cargo Headaches

Antarctica ensures nothing comes easy. NCPOR usually allows only two participants per institute, but our workflow required three: a pilot, an instrument specialist and a GPS lead. After a volley of persuasive emails—and maybe one dramatic paragraph—we secured the extra seat. The hurdles kept coming: seven days of medical tests at AIIMS, ice-safety drills with the Indo-Tibetan Border Police at Auli, and crevasse rescue practice that left my shins questioning all career choices.

Packing cargo was a saga of its own. In Antarctica there is no “quick Amazon delivery.” Every cable, spare drone arm and GNSS battery had to be logged, labelled, and cleared through customs. For a brief moment I knew the DGCA drone regulations better than my own birthday. Weeks later the pallets finally left for the icebreaker—our gear was southbound.

The Debriefing at Goa

September brought a five-day induction at NCPOR headquarters in Goa. It covered everything from marine fire drills to mayday calls, life inside Maitri Station, and the contingency plan if the chartered flight failed and the ship had to take over. At the end we collected our diplomatic passports and, suddenly, the expedition felt very real.

  • Fire and abandon-ship procedures aboard the expedition vessel
  • Station protocols—waste, water, and the infamous “Absolutely Don’ts” list
  • Emergency communications across the Southern Ocean

Taking Off

When the deployment window finally opened we hopped from Goa to Mumbai, then onward to Addis Ababa and Cape Town. Professor RAAJ would join later; for the first leg it was just me and my senior colleague P. J. Naveen Kumar shepherding a mountain of Pelican cases. As the aircraft banked out of Cape Town towards the Southern Ocean, I realised this was only the beginning. Science, adventure, and a lot of ice were about to collide.
